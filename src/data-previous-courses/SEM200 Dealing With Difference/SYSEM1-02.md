## Reading
For homework, I asked you to read the first chapter of Ryszard Kapuściński's book *The Other* (skipping the introduction). This chapter is called "Conquest and Exchange."

In this session, we're looking through the text a bit more closely, to see if we can do the following:

1. Understand in outline what the text is saying
2. Develop an understanding of the context of the text
3. Explore or critique some of the ideas in the text

## Writing exercise
So we're going to start with a ten minute free-writing exercise. This is to get you into the right mind-set for this class. 

We'll start with two very simple questions:

1. What, if anything, did you love about this text?
2. What, if anything, did you hate about this text?

Write for ten minutes.

## Loves and Hates
First we'll bracket and share a single sentence from what you have written. When you read the sentence, read verbatim from the page, but preface it with one word: either "love" or "hate":

Then we'll talk about the text in breakout groups. In each group, appoint a spokesperson to feed back to the main group. Our aim here is to think about:

1. What are the main arguments in the text?
2. Do you agree or disagree with these arguments? Why?


## Questions to Explore
 This chapter explores two different kinds of relationship with the other. We can see the other as somebody to be *conquered*, or we can see the other as somebody with whom we can conduct *exchange*.

 For your homework, I asked you think about the following questions:

1. What do you think Kapuściński means when he says, "every encounter with the Other is an enigma, an unknown quantity — I would even say a mystery."
2. Kapuściński writes from a European perspective. How does this concern with others and otherness translate into your own cultural context?
3. Our relationships with others tend to divide between curiosity (what in ancient Greek is called *philoxenia*, the fascination with the other) and fear (*xenophobia*, the fear of the other). In what ways do human societies manage this combination of fear and curiosity?
4. Albrecht von Haller writes "Nothing is better able to dispel prejudices than familiarity with many peoples of disparate customs, laws and opinions — an otherness that for the price of a minor effort teaches us to cast off what makes people different, and to regard as the voice of nature the things on which all peoples agree." What is he arguing here, and what is he saying about otherness and sameness? Do you agree?

We're going to look at these questions in groups, and feed back in the main group.
