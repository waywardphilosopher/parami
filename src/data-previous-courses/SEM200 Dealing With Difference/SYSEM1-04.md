## Overview
Kapuściński begins by talking about a number of eras that characterise European contact with non-European Others.

1. The era of merchants and envoys
2. The era of geographical "discoveries" — or of consquest, slaughter and plunder
3. The era of Enlightenment / humanism / openness
4. The present-day era, involving three turning points: anthropology, Lévinas and multiculturalism.


## Anthropology
We'll not talk in detail about anthropology, except to say that for Kapuściński, the discipline of anthropology marked a turning point because it showed that diverse cultures were much richer and more sophisticated than we imagined. It showed that we can't take the Other for granted, and that human difference is complex. And one reason it did this was through a tradition of reportage that is ethnographic writing that comes from fieldwork. But in this class, we're going to focus on the second turning point, and that is Emmanuel Lévinas.

We'll talk about multiculturalism in the next session.

## Lévinas and the Other
This passage in the text is a little bit complicated, but we're going to break it down by exploring some key ideas in Levinas's thought:

1. Responsibility
2. The face of the other
3. Infinity

I'll talk us through some of this, to see if we can make more sense of it. But the basic idea begins with the notion that our relationship with the other is primarily ethical. In encountering the other person, we *find ourselves responsible*. This responsiblity has a twofold aspect: Lévinas calls it *height* and *destitution*

*Height* is the encounter with a **demand** in the face of the other person, the fact that they have a claim on the world — and on us — that is not negotiable and that calls us into question.

*Destitution* is the encounter with a **need** in the other person. In other words, we encounter the other person who has needs that are not our needs, but are different from our own. 

Not only this, but we encounter this responsibility in the **face** of the other. This is a way Levinas has of trying to capture the strange experience of encountering *somebody else*. In our encounters with somebody else, they are not just objects in the world, but subjects just like us. They have whole worlds going on inside, but we have no access to their worlds. There's something about them that we can't fully grasp. But in encountering their face — this hard-to-pin-down but very immediate sense of them *as* another human being — we also encounter the fact that they are somehow beyond our power.

The final thing is that our responsiblity for others is **infinite**. What, you might ask, does this mean? Levinas tells us that it means that our responsibility increases the more we assume it. So you respond to the need of another, and you find that you become more responsible, not less. Imagine that one day you help your neighbour with their shopping. On the following day, it is harder to *not* help them. Responsibility only increases, the more we respond.
